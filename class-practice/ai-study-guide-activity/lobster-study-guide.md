Lobster Facts Study Guide

1. Classification
- Lobsters are crustaceans, related to crabs, shrimp, and crayfish.
- They are decapods, which means "ten legs." They have ten walking legs, and in clawed lobsters the front pair are the big claws.
- "True" or clawed lobsters belong to the family Nephropidae.
- The American lobster's scientific name is Homarus americanus. The European lobster is Homarus gammarus.
- Spiny lobsters (also called rock lobsters) are a different family, Palinuridae. They have no large claws and have long, spiny antennae instead.

2. Body and Anatomy
- A lobster has a hard outer shell called an exoskeleton. It has no internal skeleton.
- The body has two main parts: the cephalothorax (head and chest fused together) and the abdomen, which is often called the tail.
- The American lobster's two claws are different. The large, heavy crusher claw breaks shells. The thinner pincher claw (also called the cutter or seizer claw) tears and holds soft food.
- A lobster can be "right-handed" or "left-handed," depending on which side has the crusher claw.
- Lobster blood is clear to pale blue. It contains hemocyanin, a copper-based molecule that carries oxygen. Human blood uses iron-based hemoglobin.
- Lobsters taste with tiny sensory hairs on their legs and smell with their small antennae, called antennules.
- Their eyesight is poor. Their eyes are better at sensing motion in dim light than at seeing sharp images.
- Lobsters chew food in their stomach. The stomach has tooth-like grinding structures called the gastric mill.
- Lobsters release urine from openings near the base of their antennae. They squirt urine at each other as a chemical signal during fights and courtship.

3. Color
- Most live American lobsters are a dark greenish-brown or mottled color, which helps them hide on the sea floor.
- Lobsters turn bright red when cooked. Heat breaks down a protein in the shell, crustacyanin, which releases a red pigment called astaxanthin.
- Blue lobsters are rare, often estimated at about 1 in 2 million. Yellow, orange, split-colored, and white (albino) lobsters are even rarer.

4. Growth and Molting
- To grow, a lobster must shed its old exoskeleton. This is called molting.
- After molting, the new shell is soft. The lobster hides until the shell hardens.
- Young lobsters molt many times a year. Adults molt about once a year or less.
- Lobsters can regrow (regenerate) lost claws, legs, and antennae over several molts.
- American lobsters usually take about 5 to 7 years to reach legal harvest size.

5. Life Cycle and Reproduction
- Females usually mate right after they molt, while their shell is still soft.
- A female carries her eggs glued under her tail on the swimmerets. A female carrying eggs is called "berried."
- An American lobster female carries her eggs for about 9 to 11 months before they hatch.
- A large female can carry tens of thousands of eggs. Very large females can carry over 100,000.
- Hatched larvae float near the surface as plankton for several weeks. Very few survive to adulthood.
- You can tell males and females apart by the first pair of swimmerets under the tail. In males they are hard and bony. In females they are soft and feathery.

6. Behavior and Diet
- Lobsters are mostly nocturnal. They hide in burrows and rock crevices during the day and come out at night to feed.
- They are omnivores. They eat fish, clams, mussels, sea urchins, worms, other crustaceans, and some plants.
- Lobsters can be aggressive and territorial, and they may eat each other in crowded tanks.
- To escape danger, a lobster quickly curls its tail under its body, which shoots it backward through the water.
- Lobsters walk forward on the sea floor but swim backward.

7. Lifespan and Size
- Lobsters can live a long time. Scientists estimate that many American lobsters live 50 years or more.
- Age is hard to measure because lobsters shed their shells, so there are no growth rings to count.
- Lobsters are not immortal. That is a popular myth. Molting takes a lot of energy, and older lobsters can die from exhaustion during a molt or from shell disease when they stop molting.
- The heaviest lobster on record was an American lobster caught in Nova Scotia, Canada, in 1977. It weighed about 44 pounds (20 kilograms).

8. Lobsters and People
- In colonial New England, lobsters were so plentiful that they were considered cheap food for poor people, servants, and prisoners. They were even used as fertilizer.
- Lobster became a luxury food in the late 1800s and 1900s, as railroads and canning carried it to cities far from the coast.
- Maine catches more American lobster than any other U.S. state.
- Lobsters are usually caught in baited traps called lobster pots.
- In Maine, fishermen cut a small V-shaped notch into the tail flipper of egg-bearing females before releasing them. A V-notched lobster is illegal to keep, which protects breeding females.
- Maine also has minimum and maximum size limits, so both young lobsters and the largest breeders are thrown back.
